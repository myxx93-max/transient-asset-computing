# Tradition Expression Guide

This guide is an initial orientation map for contributors. It is **not** a definitive account of any religion. Traditions are internally diverse, historically changing, and described differently by practitioners, scholars, and outsiders. Each public record should be grounded in its own sources and, where possible, reviewed by people from the relevant community.

## 1. Chinese local religion, Daoist traditions, and Buddhist traditions

Do not treat “Chinese religion” as one unified pantheon. Local temple networks, lineage and household practices, Daoist ritual traditions, Buddhist institutions, regional cults, and state or community histories may overlap without being identical.

Possible terms include **神明**, **神祇**, **地方神**, **祖先**, **护佑**, **香火**, **庙宇**, **坛**, **法脉**, **科仪**, **愿**, and **灵验**. Their meanings depend on region, lineage, text, and speaker. Record the term as used; do not assume every local deity belongs to a single national hierarchy or that every temple practice is Daoist or Buddhist.

For Buddhist contexts, preserve distinctions among **Buddha**, **bodhisattva**, **arhat**, **deva**, **guardian figure**, **祖师**, and local devotional figures. A bodhisattva should not simply be relabeled as a “god”. Buddhist traditions differ substantially by school, region, and practice.

## 2. Hindu traditions

Use the community's own terms, such as **deva**, **devi**, **Ishvara**, **Brahman**, **avatar**, **murti**, and **bhakti**, only with contextual explanation. Hindu traditions include diverse devotional, philosophical, ritual, and regional forms. A figure may be understood differently across traditions and communities; some theologies emphasize one supreme reality or deity while others foreground multiple divine forms or local traditions.

Do not assume that a list of deities captures Hindu theology, or that all practitioners interpret divine plurality in the same way. Record the particular sampradaya, region, language, text, or community context where known.

## 3. Ancient Greek and Roman traditions

For Greek and Roman materials, preserve the relevant historical and linguistic setting. Names such as **Zeus/Jupiter**, **Hera/Juno**, **Athena/Minerva**, and **Ares/Mars** may be compared, but Roman religious life was not merely Greek religion with renamed gods. Interpretatio, translation, local cults, civic institutions, household religion, and historical change matter.

Use period-specific terms where sources support them, and distinguish literary depictions from cult practice, inscriptions, archaeology, and later reception. A mythological story is not automatically evidence of a uniform historical belief.

## 4. Norse and other Germanic traditions

Use source-specific terms such as **Æsir**, **Vanir**, **jötnar**, **dísir**, and **landvættir** only where relevant to the source and period. The surviving evidence is uneven and often mediated through later texts. Distinguish medieval literary sources, archaeological interpretation, modern Heathen practice, and contemporary reconstruction.

Do not treat modern practitioners as direct evidence for every detail of pre-Christian practice, nor treat medieval texts as transparent records of a single uniform religion.

## 5. Shinto and Japanese religious contexts

Preserve the Japanese term **kami** and explain it in context rather than translating every kami as “god”. Kami may be associated with places, natural features, ancestors, historical figures, objects, or other forms of sacred presence depending on the context.

Japanese religious history includes long interaction among shrine traditions, Buddhism, local cults, and institutions. Avoid projecting a sharp modern separation backward across all historical periods. Distinguish shrine practice, Buddhist materials, local customs, modern institutional categories, and individual community interpretations.

## 6. Judaism, Christianity, and Islam

These traditions are not pantheons and should not be forced into a deity catalogue. Their central theological language concerns the one God, though each tradition has its own vocabulary, doctrines, interpretive schools, and internal diversity.

Records may focus on:
- names and attributes of God as used in a particular textual or theological context;
- scripture, commentary, liturgy, prayer, and devotional practice;
- angels, prophets, saints (where recognized), revered figures, and sacred places;
- community memory, historical interpretation, and lived practice.

For Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, use tradition-specific terminology and avoid treating the divine name as just another entity node among many gods. Respect restrictions and sensitivities around names, images, representation, and sacred texts. Do not assume all communities share the same rules.

## 7. Indigenous, land-based, and local traditions

There is no single “Indigenous religion” category. Use the specific people's or community's name when it is appropriate and publicly authorized. Many traditions hold knowledge through relationships among land, ancestors, nonhuman beings, kinship, story, and practice; these relationships may not map to a deity-centered taxonomy.

Some knowledge is restricted, seasonal, gendered, initiated, family-held, or not intended for public circulation. Do not collect or publish it merely because it can be found or recorded. Community authority, consent, data sovereignty, and withdrawal protocols take precedence over the project's desire for completeness.

## 8. Sikh, Jain, Buddhist, and other traditions not reducible to a pantheon

Do not presume that every tradition organizes its central teachings around creator gods or divine beings. For example, records in Sikh contexts may focus on **Ik Onkar**, the Guru tradition, scripture, and devotional practice; Jain contexts may focus on **Tirthankaras**, teachings, liberation, and lineage; Buddhist contexts may focus on Buddhas, bodhisattvas, teachings, practices, and cosmologies without treating a creator deity as central.

These are only orientation examples. Contributors should supply the relevant school, lineage, language, community, and source context.

## 9. Contributor language rules

Prefer:
- “In this community's account…”
- “This source describes…”
- “The term is translated here as…”
- “Some practitioners understand…”
- “This comparison is proposed by [named source/contributor]…”
- “The relationship remains disputed/unknown.”

Avoid:
- “All cultures worship the same god under different names.”
- “This deity is obviously equivalent to…”
- “This is the true origin of…”
- “This religion is more advanced/primitive.”
- “This figure ranks above/below…”
- “The tradition believes…” when only one source or one contributor is available.

## 10. Invitation

We welcome contributors who can explain the terms, distinctions, and boundaries of their own tradition. A correction that says “our tradition should not be described this way” is as valuable as a new entry.
